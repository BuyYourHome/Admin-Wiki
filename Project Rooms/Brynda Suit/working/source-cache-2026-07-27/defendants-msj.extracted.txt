   INTRODUCTION This lawsuit is an attempt to unwind the sale-and-leaseback transaction that Plaintiff Brynda Griffith knowingly pursued and executed to stop the foreclosure of her home. Seeking recission, Plaintiff recites claims of fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, unfair and deceptive trade practices, and alternative claims of undue influence and lack of mental capacity. None can survive summary judgment. The undisputed evidence shows that Plaintiff understood the foreclosure, evaluated alternatives, contacted the Defendants, negotiated the deal that allowed her to stay in her home, and executed the documents herself. There is no evidence of deception, control, misuse of a power of attorney, or lack of capacity—nor could there be, given that Plaintiff is prosecuting this action on her own behalf.  Because there is no genuine dispute of any material fact, Defendants are entitled to judgment as a matter of law on all claims.

GOVERNING STANDARD Summary judgment is properly granted if the pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, admissions, and affidavits show the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. N.C. Gen. Stat. § 1A-1, Rule 56(c). A defendant is entitled to summary judgment when discovery shows that a plaintiff cannot produce evidence to support an essential element of their claim. Draughon v. Harnett Cnty. Bd. of Educ., 158 N.C. App. 208, 212, 580 S.E.2d 732, 735 (2003), aff’d, 358 N.C. 131, 591 S.E.2d 521 (2004).

ARGUMENT I. Summary judgment is warranted on the four claims premised on breach of fiduciary duty because Plaintiff conveyed the property herself, outside any fiduciary relationship. Plaintiff’s claims for breach of fiduciary duty (Count 1), constructive fraud (Count 3), civil conspiracy (Count 4), and declaratory judgment (Count 10) all rest on the same erroneous premise: that Mr. Browning breached a fiduciary duty by using a power of attorney given by Plaintiff to convey the Property on Plaintiff’s behalf to his own trust. Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that Mr. Browning “exercised his fiduciary authority to facilitate the conveyance of the Property on behalf of Plaintiff, selling the Property for well below fair market value,” thus “benefitting himself.” Am. Compl. ¶ 25 (emphasis added); see also id. ¶ 26 (alleging that “[b]y using his authority to facilitate this transaction, Mr. Browning used his position as a fiduciary for his personal benefit”); id. ¶¶ 37–42 (same) (constructive fraud); id. ¶¶ 47–48 (same) (civil conspiracy); id. ¶ 72 (same) (declaratory judgment).  It is axiomatic that “[f]or a breach of fiduciary duty to exist, there must first be a fiduciary relationship between the parties.” Duffy v. Camp, 287 N.C. App. 46, 60, 882 S.E.2d 675, 689–90 (2022) (quoting Dalton v. Camp, 353 N.C. 647, 651, 548 S.E.2d 704, 707 (2001)). Notably here, the only fiduciary relationship Plaintiff alleges is the one arising out of the power of attorney. Thus, for there to be grounds for Plaintiff to allege a breach of fiduciary duty—one arising out of the sale of real property—the agent must have acted on behalf of their principal in the sale. In other words, no breach of fiduciary duty claim lies unless Mr. Browning exercised his authority as Plaintiff’s attorney-in-fact to convey the Property on Plaintiff’s 
===== PDF PAGE 11 =====
 11 
behalf. As explained more fully below, the undisputed evidence establishes that that is not the case. Instead, Plaintiff conveyed the Property on her own behalf. On their face, all the documents related to the sale of the property including the Seller’s Acknowledgment, the Purchase and Sales Agreement, and the Deed, plainly contradict Plaintiff’s core allegation that Mr. Browning conveyed the Property on Plaintiff’s behalf. Mr. Browning signed the documents for the buyer. He did not, however, sign any of those documents on behalf of Plaintiff, the seller. Rather, Plaintiff signed each document on her own behalf. See Browning Aff., Exs. 3, 4, 8; see also Ex. C (Huston Dep.) at 63:13–65:19 (testifying that Plaintiff “signed [the purchase and sales agreement and the deed] herself” and “conclud[ing] that there is no agent who has signed for Brynda Griffith”). Plaintiff likewise entered into the leaseback agreement on her own behalf. Id., Ex. 5. In addition, all Plaintiff’s signatures were notarized.  The simple, indisputable fact that she executed the sale herself is fatal to all of four of Plaintiff’s claims predicated on an alleged breach of a fiduciary duty.  Plaintiff’s attempts to plead around her own execution of the sales documents are futile. Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that Mr. Browning “facilitated” the sale-and-leaseback transaction. However, the fact that Mr. Browning may have facilitated the transaction by, among other things, preparing the documents, hiring a notary, and recording a deed does not implicate a fiduciary duty and cannot establish breach. Indeed, this transaction was no different in that respect from any other

residential real estate transaction or consumer commercial transaction in which one party takes the lead on handling certain aspects of the transaction.   Further, Plaintiff cannot prove that Mr. Browning used his position as attorney-in-fact to “influence” Plaintiff to sell the Property at an allegedly discounted price. See Am. Compl. ¶ 40. The power of attorney did not preexist the negotiations or Plaintiff’s execution of the sales agreement. Rather, the power of attorney was signed in conjunction with the sale of the Property, in case Mr. Browning needed it to communicate with the bank to pay the debt and halt the foreclosure. See Browning Aff. ¶¶ 26–27. II. Summary judgment is appropriate on Plaintiff’s claims of fraud and unfair and deceptive trade practices because the record contains no evidence of deception or misrepresentation. Plaintiff’s claims for fraud (Count 2) and unfair and deceptive trade practices (Count 9) both rest on the core allegation that Mr. Browning concealed material information from Plaintiff to induce her to enter the sale-and-leaseback transaction. In a key allegation, Plaintiff alleges that Mr. Browning committed fraud by concealing material facts and coercing her to sign documents under the “guise that [Defendants] were helping Plaintiff get up to date on the obligations of the Property.” See, e.g., Am. Compl. ¶ 31. While Plaintiff fails to specifically allege what, if any, material facts were concealed from her, she alleges that she believed Mr. Browning was (merely) loaning her the money to pay off the equity line debt. Id. ¶ 18. She further alleges that the Defendants’ conduct constitutes unfair or deceptive trade practices. Id. ¶¶ 66–69.  Allegations of fraud and deception require more particularity than what is set forth in Plaintiff’s amended complaint, but at the summary judgment stage, even well-pleaded allegations are not enough. These claims require evidentiary support to create an issue of material fact.

“The essential elements of fraud are: (1) [f]alse representation or concealment of a . . . material fact, (2) reasonably calculated to deceive, (3) made with intent to deceive, (4) which does in fact deceive, (5) resulting in damage to the injured party.” See, e.g., Hardin v. KCS Int’l, Inc., 199 N.C. App. 687, 696, 682 S.E.2d 726, 733 (2009) (quoting Phelps–Dickson Builders, L.L.C. v. Amerimann Partners, 172 N.C. App. 427, 437, 617 S.E.2d 664, 670 (2005)). Plaintiff must produce evidence of each of these elements in order to survive summary judgment. Similarly, a voluntarily negotiated transaction, like the one here, requires evidence of deceptive or coercive conduct to avoid summary judgment on an unfair or deceptive trade practice claim. Bumpers v. Cmty. Bank of N. Va., 367 N.C. 81, 91–92, 747 S.E.2d 220, 228 (2013). As discussed more fully below, no genuine issue of material fact exists. Summary judgment is therefore warranted on Counts 2 and 9. A. Plaintiff’s argument that this is a “foreclosure rescue transaction” fails as a matter of law.  In support of her motion for summary judgment, Plaintiff contends that the sale-and-leaseback constitutes a “foreclosure rescue transaction” under N.C. Gen. Stat. § 75-120(3), subjecting Defendants to the safe-harbor requirements of § 75-121. See Pl. Supp. Br. pp. 7–8. But the transaction does not meet the statutory definition. A “foreclosure rescue transaction” requires, among other elements, that “[t]he transferor retains a tenancy interest, an interest under a lease with option to purchase agreement, or an option to reacquire the property.” N.C. Gen. Stat. § 75-120(3)(d). The operative word is “retains.” Under well-established North Carolina law, to “retain” an interest in property is to hold back or reserve something from the conveyance—to except it from the transfer so that it never passes to the grantee in 
===== PDF PAGE 17 =====
 17 
the first place. See, e.g., Sparks v. Choate, 22 N.C. App. 62, 63, 205 S.E.2d 624, 625 (1974); Vance v. Pritchard, 213 N.C. 552, 197 S.E. 182 (1938); see also Retain, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (“To hold in possession or under control; to keep and not lose, part with, or dismiss.”). In other words, a retained interest is one that remains with the grantor because the instrument of conveyance did not include it. See Sparks, 22 N.C. App. at 63, 205 S.E.2d at 625. Sparks is instructive. There, the plaintiff and other co-tenants conveyed a tract of land by “general warranty deed, without exception.” Id. at 62–63, 205 S.E.2d at 625. The plaintiff warranted “that the land was free and clear of all encumbrances, and that plaintiff would defend title to the tract against the claim of all persons whomsoever.” Id. at 63, 205 S.E.2d at 625. The plaintiff later attempted to claim an easement of right of way across the conveyed property, relying on a reservation in an 1898 deed from an independent source. Id. The Court of Appeals held that the plaintiff was estopped from “seeking to retain an interest in property which he had heretofore conveyed with full warranty.” Id. The principle is straightforward: once a grantor conveys property by full warranty without reserving a particular interest, the grantor has parted with the entire estate and cannot later claim to have retained that interest. That principle controls here, albeit in a different posture. Plaintiff conveyed the Property by general warranty deed, covenanting that the Property was “free and clear of all encumbrances” and warranting that she would “defend the title 
===== PDF PAGE 18 =====
 18 
against the lawful claims of all persons whomsoever, other than the following exceptions: easements, covenants, and restrictions of record.”  
 Browning Aff., Ex. 8 (Deed).  To be sure, the deed here is not entirely “without exception” as in Sparks—it contains the standard title exception for recorded encumbrances. But that exception is not a reservation of a tenancy interest, a possessory right, or any other interest that Plaintiff held back for herself. It is a conventional qualifier acknowledging preexisting encumbrances that run with the land—nothing more. The deed contains no language reserving a life estate, a term of years, a right of reentry, or any other “tenancy interest” in favor of the grantor. Plaintiff conveyed the full fee simple estate and retained nothing. Only after the conveyance was complete did Plaintiff enter into a separate lease agreement under which Defendants—as the new owners of the fee—granted Plaintiff a tenancy interest in the Property. See Browning Aff., Ex. 5 (Lease Agreement). That tenancy interest was not carved out of Plaintiff's original estate and held back; it was a new interest, independently created by the Defendants and conferred upon Plaintiff by a separate instrument. In sum, Plaintiff did not “retain” a tenancy interest within the meaning of § 75-120(3)(d). She relinquished all of her 

===== PDF PAGE 19 =====
 19 
property interests and then received a new and independent possessory interest from the Defendants. Furthermore, the General Assembly’s use of the word “retains” was deliberate. The legislature knew how to describe a possessory interest that arises after a transfer—indeed, subsection (3)(d) itself separately identifies “an interest under a lease with option to purchase agreement,” recognizing a distinct category of post-transfer arrangement. Had the General Assembly intended subsection (3)(d) to reach any arrangement in which a transferor continues to occupy the property after a conveyance, regardless of how that occupancy interest arose, it could have said so. It could have written “the transferor continues to occupy the property,” or “the transferor obtains a tenancy interest,” or “the transferor possesses a tenancy interest following the transfer.” It did none of these things. Instead, it chose a term with established meaning in property law—“retains”—and this Court should give that term its ordinary legal significance. See State v. Dye, 139 N.C. App. 148, 152, 532 S.E.2d 574, 577 (2000) (“In interpreting statutory language, it is presumed the General Assembly intended the words it used to have the meaning they have in ordinary speech, and when the plain meaning is unambiguous, a court should go no further in interpreting the statute than its ordinary meaning.”) (quotations and citations omitted). Moreover, Plaintiff’s reading violates the rule against surplusage. If “tenancy interest” encompassed all leases, the second prong of subsection (3)(d)—“an interest under a lease with option to purchase agreement”—would be entirely superfluous 
===== PDF PAGE 20 =====
 20 
because a lease-option is itself a lease. See State v. Morgan, 372 N.C. 609, 614, 831 S.E.2d 254, 258 (2019) (explaining “that a statute may not be interpreted in a manner which would render any of its words superfluous”). The only reading of subsection (3)(d) that gives each prong independent meaning is if “tenancy interest” refers to a possessory interest retained by the transferor in the conveyance itself, while “an interest under a lease with option to purchase agreement” refers to a distinct contractual arrangement.  Accordingly, the Court should reject Plaintiff’s attempt to reframe this transaction as a “foreclosure rescue.” 
III. Summary judgment is appropriate on Plaintiff’s alternative mental-incapacity claims because Plaintiff is estopped from asserting them and offers no supporting evidence. Plaintiff’s claim in the alternative for mental incompetency or incapacity (Count 7) fails as a matter of law for two independent reasons. First, Plaintiff is estopped under North Carolina law from challenging the validity of those documents on incapacity grounds while prosecuting this action on her own behalf. But even if her claim was not estopped, it would fail for a second, independent reason: Plaintiff has produced no evidence that she lacked the mental capacity to execute the deed conveying the Property or enter the sale-and-leaseback transaction.  A. Plaintiff is estopped from asserting lack of capacity or incompetency. Plaintiff’s incapacity claim fails because she is estopped from asserting it. North Carolina courts have long held that a party who prosecutes an action in her

own name is estopped from contending that she lacked mental capacity to execute a prior conveyance. See Davis v. Davis, 223 N.C. 36, 38, 25 S.E.2d 181, 183 (1943). As the Supreme Court of North Carolina explained in Davis, a litigant who “is mentally competent to assert h[er] rights and protect h[er] interest at the present time . . . is estopped from challenging the validity of [the deed in question].” Id. Here, Plaintiff is prosecuting this action on her own behalf, with counsel, seeking affirmative relief. See Am. Compl. Having done so, she cannot repudiate the legal capacity required to execute the sale-and-leaseback transaction at issue. Plaintiff’s claim for mental incompetency or incapacity therefore fails as a matter of law. See Davis, 223 N.C. at 38, 25 S.E.2d at 183. B. There is no evidence that Plaintiff lacked capacity or was incompetent. Even if Plaintiff were not estopped from challenging the transaction for lack of capacity, her claims would still fail. Under North Carolina law, the capacity to execute a deed requires that the grantor: “(1) comprehend[ ] the natural objects of his bounty, (2) understand[ ] the kind, nature and extent of his property, (3) know[ ] the manner in which he desires his act to take effect, and (4) realize[ ] the effect his act will have upon his estate.” Jones v. Corn, 293 N.C. App. 596, 604, 902 S.E.2d 17, 24 (2024). The burden of proving lack of mental capacity rests with the party asserting it. Ridings v. Ridings, 55 N.C. App. 630, 633, 286 S.E.2d 614, 616 (1982), disc. rev. denied, 305 N.C. 586, 292 S.E.2d 571 (1982). Plaintiff has failed to meet that burden. The undisputed record shows that Plaintiff understood her home was in foreclosure, appreciated that she could lose 
===== PDF PAGE 22 =====
 22 
the property if the foreclosure proceeded, discussed available options, reviewed written transaction documents, and executed those documents herself in the presence of a notary. Nothing in the record—medical or otherwise—suggests that Plaintiff failed to comprehend the nature, purpose, or effect of the deed or related agreements at the time she signed them. See Jones, 293 N.C. App. at 604, 902 S.E.2d at 24 (stating that a lack-of-capacity claim requires “evidence concerning the grantor’s capacity when the grantor executed the deed”). Plaintiff’s incapacity claim is further undermined by undisputed evidence of her legal functioning both before and after the challenged transaction. In 2019, Plaintiff, as executor of her sister’s estate, retained attorney Kevin Huston to represent the estate. Ex. C (Huston Dep.) at 10:25–11:7. While Mr. Huston had concerns over the years that certain estate tasks were “difficult” for Plaintiff, Mr. Huston never identified a conflict between Plaintiff as executor and the beneficiaries of the estate. Id. at 27:6-19; 28:5-24.  In short, Mr. Huston made the repeated determination that Plaintiff was able to fulfill her fiduciary duties to the estate beneficiaries. Accordingly, he took no action to remove Plaintiff as executor. Id. at 27:6-19 (explaining that he would have consulted a fellow attorney if he had suspected that Plaintiff was unable to continue to serve as executor); see also N.C. Gen. Stat. § 28A-9-1 (providing the grounds and means to remove the executor of an estate). And Plaintiff—with the estate’s attorney’s continued approval—proceeded to administer the estate between her 
===== PDF PAGE 23 =====

appointment as executor in 2019 through the execution of the sale-and-leaseback transaction in October 2023. Ex. C (Huston Dep.) at 10:25–11:7; 27:6-19–28:25. In addition, in September 2024—almost a year after executing the deed and sale-and-leaseback agreements—Plaintiff executed a will in the presence of Mr. Huston, who determined that Plaintiff possessed testamentary capacity. Ex. C (Huston Dep.) at 69:5–71:22.  Although testamentary capacity is a lower standard than the capacity required to execute a deed or contract, Plaintiff’s later execution of a will is nevertheless relevant.7 It confirms that Plaintiff continued to function independently in legally significant decision-making and undermines the allegation that she was broadly mentally incompetent at the time of the sale-and-leaseback transaction. See Jones, 293 N.C. App. at 604, 902 S.E.2d at 24–25 (“General statements about a grantor’s deteriorating health, alone, are insufficient to show a lack of capacity.”). This is the same independent functioning and decision-making that Mr. Browning witnessed in the year following the transaction. Browning Aff. ¶¶ 36–43. Plaintiff has offered no evidence of any intervening recovery, treatment, or change in condition between October 2023 and September 2024 that would reconcile her incapacity claim with her subsequent execution of estate-planning documents.

IV. Summary judgment is appropriate on Plaintiff’s alternative claim of undue influence because the record contains no evidence that the sale and leaseback transaction was the result of “fear or force.” The record also forecloses Plaintiff’s alternative undue influence claim (Count 8). Undue influence is “the exercise of an improper influence over the mind and will of another to such an extent that his professed act is not that of a free agent, but in reality is the act of the third person who procured the result.” Yurek v. Shaffer, 198 N.C. App. 67, 80, 678 S.E.2d 738, 746–47 (2009) (quoting Stephenson v. Warren, 136 N.C. App. 768, 772, 525 S.E.2d 809, 812 (2000)). To establish undue influence, a plaintiff must show “(1) a person who is subject to influence; (2) an opportunity to exert influence; (3) a disposition to exert influence; and (4) a result indicating undue influence.” In re Will of Dunn, 129 N.C. App. 321, 328, 500 S.E.2d 99, 104 (1998). To survive summary judgment, a plaintiff must “present sufficient evidence to demonstrate [defendant] had exerted influence on [the grantor] to the extent that ‘the end reached is tantamount to the effect produced by the use of fear or force.’” In re Estate of Blue, No. COA23-505, 2024 WL 158257, at *6 (N.C. Ct. App. Jan. 16, 2024) (unpublished) (quoting In re Will of Jones, 362 N.C. 569, 574, 669 S.E.2d 572, 577 (2008)). Where, like here, “there is no confidential or fiduciary relationship, the burden of proof remains upon the party alleging undue influence.” Curl v. Key, 64 N.C. App. 139, 143, 306 S.E.2d 818, 821 (1983), rev’d on other grounds, 311 N.C. 
===== PDF PAGE 25 =====
 25 
259, 316 S.E.2d 272 (1984). As described more fully below, Plaintiff has not carried that burden.  A.  Plaintiff has not shown she was subject to influence. North Carolina law presumes that every individual has the capacity to transact business “in the absence of evidence to the contrary.” See Davis, 223 N.C. at 38 25 S.E.2d at183. Plaintiff here offers no evidence to the contrary. Specifically, Plaintiff has identified no evidence of cognitive impairment, mental illness, physical infirmity, or diminished capacity affecting her at the time of the transaction.  Moreover, the evidence shows that at the time of the transaction, Plaintiff understood the precarious situation she faced in foreclosure and proactively sought out offers to buy the Property. See supra at pp 3–4. After considering at least one other offer, Plaintiff executed the transaction documents in the presence of a notary and raised no concern that she was acting under pressure or against her will. See Herrin v. Herrin, No. COA10-1367, 2011 WL 2848847, at *3 (N.C. Ct. App. July 19, 2011) (unpublished) (giving weight to the fact that plaintiff did not indicate to the notary that she was not exercising her own free will).  Plaintiff’s age alone, without more, cannot create a triable issue. See Curl, 64 N.C. App. at 143, 306 S.E.2d at 821 (finding that grantors were not susceptible to undue influence where there was no showing that they were “physically or mentally disabled in any way”).    
===== PDF PAGE 26 =====
 26 
B.  Plaintiff has not shown Mr. Browning had the opportunity to exert influence.  The record is devoid of any evidence that Mr. Browning had the type of relationship with Plaintiff that would have provided the access, proximity, or position of trust necessary to overpower her free will. See Andrews, 299 N.C. at 55, 261 S.E.2d at 200 (identifying as relevant factors whether the grantor was “in the home of the beneficiary and subject to [the beneficiary’s] constant association and supervision” and whether “others ha[d] little or no opportunity to see” the grantor). Mr. Browning did not live with Plaintiff, manage her affairs, control her access to family or advisors, or occupy any position of special trust or responsibility. Cf. Curl, 64 N.C. App. at 143, 306 S.E.2d at 821 (finding no confidential relationship where the defendant “was not a blood relative,” and “occupied no position of special trust or responsibility as to the plaintiffs”).  Instead, Mr. Browning’s acquaintance with Plaintiff was brief, recent, and purely transactional—spanning only 3 days. Browning Aff. ¶¶ 9–10, 20, 25. In total, Mr. Browning spent no more than an hour and forty-five minutes with Plaintiff before she executed the sale and leaseback documents. Id. ¶¶ 20–24. Such limited, arm’s-length contact is insufficient to establish that Mr. Browning substituted his will for Plaintiff’s or controlled her decision-making process.  C.  Plaintiff has not shown a disposition to exert influence. Plaintiff asks the Court to infer wrongful intent from the mere fact that a transaction occurred, but the law does not permit such an inference. Dunn, 129 N.C. 
===== PDF PAGE 27 =====
 27 
App. 321 at 328, 500 S.E.2d at 104 (requiring affirmative evidence of opportunity, disposition, and actual exercise of influence over the grantor’s free will). There is nothing in the record suggesting that Mr. Browning sought to manipulate Plaintiff, discouraged her from obtaining independent advice, made misrepresentations about the Property’s value or Plaintiff’s alternatives, or otherwise engaged in conduct aimed at overcoming her free agency.  D.  The sale-and-leaseback, under the threat of foreclosure for Plaintiff who had nowhere else to go, does not reflect a result indicating undue influence.  Finally, the sale price does not give rise to a “result indicating undue influence” when considered in context—that Plaintiff faced foreclosure, had nowhere else to go, and wanted to stay in her home. Dunn, 129 N.C. App. 321 at 328, 500 S.E.2d at 104. A property owner facing foreclosure has an obvious and independent reason to accept a below-market price: to salvage whatever equity remains before the lender forecloses and the owner receives nothing or faces a deficiency. In other words, a discounted sale price is not evidence of an overborne will; it is the predictable consequence of a distressed sale. Cf. Curl, 64 N.C. App. at 142–44, 306 S.E.2d at 820–21 (considering whether independent consideration existed for the transaction and whether the circumstances otherwise explained the transfer).   The additional context here is that Plaintiff wanted to stay in her home, and Mr. Browning provided her a way to do that, as he had other elderly sellers. See Browning Aff. ¶¶ 3, 12. The discounted price must be considered alongside the 
===== PDF PAGE 28 =====
 28 
generous terms of rent for life Mr. Browning gave Plaintiff, for the rest of her life if she desired. In sum, Plaintiff’s evidence amounts to an elderly person selling her home at a discount while facing foreclosure—a distressed sale, not undue influence. Plaintiff has failed to forecast evidence that Mr. Browning exerted any influence over her, let alone influence “tantamount to the effect produced by the use of fear or force.” Blue, 2024 WL 158257, at *6. While the circumstances of facing foreclosure and no alternative living arrangements certainly could cause some fear for Plaintiff, Mr. Browning was not the cause. To the contrary, he offered her a way out—avoid foreclosure and stay for life if desired at minimal rent. Summary judgment is therefore appropriate on Count 8. 
V. Summary judgment is appropriate on the remaining claims for quiet title, punitive damages, and declaratory judgment because Plaintiff’s underlying claims fail as a matter of law. Plaintiff’s remaining claims—for quiet title (Count 5), punitive damages (Count 6), and declaratory judgment (Count 10)—are not independent causes of action. Each is expressly dependent on the existence of underlying unlawful conduct. Under North Carolina law, when the substantive claims fail, derivative claims seeking to undo a transaction, punish defendants, or obtain declaratory relief necessarily fail as well. See, e.g., Bryant v. Wake Forest Univ. Baptist Med. Ctr., 281 N.C. App. 630, 645, 870 S.E.2d 269, 279 (2022) (“[P]unitive damages do not and cannot exist as an independent cause of action, but are mere incidents of the cause of action.”); Abitol v. Clark, 296 N.C. App. 557, 561, 910 S.E.2d 10, 13 (2024) 
===== PDF PAGE 29 =====
 29 
(stating that a “controversy must exist between the interested parties both at the time of filing and at the time of hearing at which the matter comes before the trial court”). Summary judgment is appropriate on Counts 5, 6, and 10. A.  Quiet Title. Under N.C. Gen. Stat. § 41-10, any person claiming an interest in real property may bring an action to determine adverse claims and quiet title. See N.C. Gen. Stat. § 41-10. Where the challenged conveyance is valid and no underlying wrongdoing is shown, there is no basis to disturb legal title. See Walter v. Walter, 275 N.C. App. 956, 966, 853 S.E.2d 318, 325 (2020) (explaining that a quiet title action requires an “underlying theory of relief”). Here, Plaintiff’s quiet title claim depends entirely on her allegations of breach of fiduciary duty, fraud, undue influence, and incapacity. As shown above, those claims fail as a matter of law. The undisputed evidence likewise establishes that Plaintiff executed the deed herself, in her own name and on her own behalf. In the absence of any invalidity in the conveyance, Plaintiff cannot quiet title against Defendants, and summary judgment is appropriate on Count 5. B.  Punitive Damages. Punitive damages are not a standalone cause of action under North Carolina law. Rather, they are available only upon proof of an underlying tort accompanied by aggravating factors such as fraud, malice, or willful or wanton conduct. See N.C. Gen. Stat. § 1D-15(a); see also Iadanza v. Harper, 169 N.C. App. 776, 783, 611 
===== PDF PAGE 30 =====
 30 
S.E.2d 217, 223 (2005) (“If the injured party has no cause of action independent of a supposed right to recover punitive damages, then he has no cause of action at all.”). Because Plaintiff cannot establish any underlying tort, her claim for punitive damages necessarily fails, and Count 6 must be dismissed as a matter of law. C.  Declaratory Judgment. The North Carolina Declaratory Judgment Act, N.C. Gen. Stat. § 1-253 et seq., does not create substantive rights; it provides a procedural vehicle for resolving actual controversies where legal rights are genuinely in dispute. Abitol, 296 N.C. App. at 561, 910 S.E.2d at 13. Where a declaratory claim merely restates failed substantive theories, no actual controversy remains for resolution, and summary judgment is appropriate. See id. Here, Plaintiff’s declaratory judgment claim seeks a declaration invalidating the same transaction she challenges through her substantive causes of action. Because those claims fail on the undisputed facts, no actual controversy remains for declaratory relief. Summary judgment is therefore warranted on Count 10.
