ARGUMENT 
 Under Rule 56 of the North Carolina Rules of Civil Procedure, summary judgment is 
proper if the pleadings and other record materials “show that there is no genuine issue as to any 
material fact” and a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. N.C. Gen. Stat. § 1A-1, Rule 
56. The party moving for summary judgment bears the initial burden of showing the lack of any 
triable issue. Collingwood v. Gen. Elec. Real Estate Equities , 324 N.C. 63, 66, 376 S.E.2d 425, 
427 (1989). Once the moving party satisfies this initial burden, “then the burden shifts to the non-
moving party to ‘set forth specific facts showing that there is a genuine issue for trial.’” Lowe v. 
Bradford, 305 N.C. 366, 369- 70, 289 S.E.2d 363, 366 (1982). An issue is “material” if the facts 
alleged would affect the result of the action, or its resolution would prevent the party moving for 
summary judgment from prevailing. DeWitt v. Eveready Battery Co. , 355 N.C. 672, 681, 565 
S.E.2d 140, 146 (2002). The non- movant must come forward with affidavits showing that a 
material factual dispute exists and may not rely on conclusory allegations. Jones v. Columbus 
County Hosp., 157 N.C. App.716, 580 S.E.2d 97 (2003) (citing Ellis v. White , 156 N.C. App. 16, 
575 S.E.2d 809 (2003)). The evidence must be evaluated in the light most favorable to the non-
moving party. Sumney v. Barker , 357 N.C. 492, 496, 586 S .E.2d  247, 249 (2003). Thus, in 
evaluating Ms. Griffith’s Motion for Summary Judgment, the evidence must be evaluated in the 
light most favorable to Defendant, and in evaluating Defendant’s Cross -Motion for Summary 
Judgment, the evidence must be evaluated in the light most favorable to Ms. Griffith.

A. Defendants entered into a Home Foreclosure Rescue Transaction with Ms. Griffith. 
Unless certain protective measures are taken  in favor of a transferor,  Home Foreclosure 
Rescue Transactions are expressly prohibited under North Carolina law. See N.C.G.S. § 75-121. 
The General Assembly has defined a Foreclosure Rescue Transaction as follows:  
(3)  Foreclosure rescue transaction. --  A transfer of residential real property, 
including a manufactured home that is permanently attached to the real property, 
which includes all of the following features:  
a. The real property is the principal residence of the transferor.  
b.  [Repealed as to transaction occurring after October 1, 2015] 
c. The transferee, an agent of the transferee, or others acting in concert 
with the transferee make representations that the transfer of the 
residential property will enable the transferor to prevent, postpone, 
or reverse the effect of foreclosure and to remain in the residence. 
d. The transferor retains a tenancy interest, an interest under a lease 
with option to purchase agreement, or an option to reacquire the 
property.  
N.C. Gen. Stat. § 120. In the present case, Defendants operated a Foreclosure Rescue Transaction. 
First, the Property was the principal residence of  Ms. Griffith, the transferor as evidenced by the 
Deed conveying the property. Second, Defendant and the related E ntities made certain 
representations to Ms. Griffith that such a transfer would “prevent, postpone, or reverse” the 
foreclosure and allow her to remain there. Defendant stated in one letter that “[t]here are a number

B. Defendants did not use the proper procedures to except this current transaction from 
a prohibited Foreclosure Rescue Transa ctions as outlined by the North Carolina

“It is unlawful for a person or entity other than the transferor to engage in, promise to 
engage in, arrange, offer, promote, solicit, assist with, or carry out a foreclosure rescue transaction 
for financial gain or with the expectation of financial gain.” N.C. Gen. Stat. § 75-121(a). However, 
the General Assembly created certain defined  exceptions to allow for such transactions to occur 
where certain protective measures are implemented for the good of the transferor. Id.  
The first protective measure is that the transferee must pay the transferor “at least fifty 
percent (50%) of the fair market value of the property as determined by a certified appraiser.” Id. 
Here, Ms. Griffith’s perceived benefit of the purchase price was $22,257.08, which was the amount 
of the HELOC loan plus $100.00 in earnest money . However, Defendant has listed the property

In Defendants’ Cross-Motion for Summary Judgment, the Defendants write that they are 
entitled to judgment as a matter of law because “Plaintiff conveyed the property in her own name, 
on her own behalf , not through Defendant Browning”  (emphasis in original). However, this 
misstates the applicable law with respect to fiduciary duties and constructive fraud.  
The claims of breach of fiduciary duty and constructive fraud are comprised of very similar 
elements.24 A plaintiff is entitled to relief on a claim of breach of fiduciary duty where the 
following circumstances are present:  
“To establish a claim for breach of fiduciary duty, claimants are required to produce 
evidence that (1) [the] defendant[] owed them a fiduciary duty of care; (2) [the] defendant[] 
violated [his] fiduciary duty; and (3) this breach of duty was a proximate cause of injury to 
[the] plaintiffs.”  
Smith v. Smith, 272 N.C. App. 539, 546, 846 S.E.2d 819, 825 (2020) (quoting Frech Broad Place, 
LLC v. Asheville Sav. Bank, S.S.B., 259 N.C. App. 769, 787, 816 S.E.2d 886, 899 (2018)). Quite 
similarly, a plaintiff is entitled to relief on a claim of breach of constructive fraud “where a 
confidential or fiduciary relationship exists, which has led up to and surrounded the consummation 
of the transaction in which [the] defendant is alleged to have taken advantage of his position of 
trust to the hurt of [the] plaintiff.” Smi th at 546, 846 S.E.2d at 825 (quoting Forbis v. Neal , 361 
N.C. 519, 528, 649 S.E.2d 382, 388 (2007)). While undue influence is a somewhat different claim 
concerning the overcoming of a person’s will to make their own decision, such a claim —much 
like breach of fiduciary duty and constructive fraud—is also subject to a rebuttable presumption

A fiduciary relationship may arise by law or by fact. Where a principal signs a Power of 
Attorney in favor of an Agent, the Agent is a fiduciary as a matter of law. Such an agent owes the 
principal certain duties within the scope of their fiduciary duty. However, a fiduciary relationship, 
even one that does not arise as a matter of a legal relationship such as a power of attorney, may 
still be created by the facts and circumstances that create a relation of trust and confidence. See  
Collier v. Bryant, 216 N.C. App. 419, 432, 719 S.E.2d 70, 81. A fiduciary relationship, moreover, 
is defined as 
one in which there has been a special confidence reposed in one who in equity and 
good conscience is bound to act in good faith and with due regard to the interests 
of the one reposing confidence . . . , [and] it extends to any possible case in which 
a fiduciary relationship exists in fact, and in which there is confidence reposed on 
one side, and resulting domination and influence on the other. 
Albert v. Cowart, 219 N.C. App. 546, 554, 727 S.E.2d 564, 570 (2012).  
In the present case, Defendant acted as a fiduciary—both as a matter of law and as a matter 
of fact. Regardless of how the relationship formed, substantial evidence shows that such a 
relationship existed.  
A. A fiduciary relationship existed between Ms. Griffith and Defendant as a Matter of 
Law because of the execution of a Power of Attorney in which Ms. Griffith named 
Defendant as her Attorney-in-Fact, the scope of which includes the conveyance of the 
Property.  
As a matter of law, a fiduciary relationship exists within “all legal relations.” Abbitt v. 
Gregory, 201 N.C. 577, 598, 160 S.E.2d 896, 906 (1931). This includes an agent under a Power 
of Attorney. See id.; see also Albert at 554, 727 S.E.2d at 570.

 The commencement of such a relationship, as a matter of law, creates a fiduciary

 Accordingly, as a matter of law based on the document itself and Defendant’s admissions, 
a fiduciary relationship existed at the time the deed was executed in favor of Defendants.

Such relationships are created by the facts and circumstances surrounding a transaction.

Moreover, in Curl, the plaintiffs filed a lawsuit to set aside a deed to property that plaintiffs 
had conveyed to the defendant. Id. In that case, the defendant was a family friend of the plaintiffs 
who “offered to help plaintiffs with their problems” after the death of their father. Id. at 262, 613 
S.E.2d at 274. All they needed to do was “sign a paper” that would give him the right to evict 
anyone from the property. Id. However, rather than granting him that exact authority, the plaintiffs 
unknowingly signed a deed of the property over to the defendant. Id. While no fiduciary 
relationship existed as a matter of law, the Supreme Court stated a similar holding to that in Abbitt, 
finding that the trial court erred by failing to apply the applicable law to fiduciary and confidential 
relationships: “‘Such a relationship exists in all cases  where there has been a special confidence

Link, 278 N.C. 181, 192, 179 S.E.2d 697, 704 (1971) (internal citations omitted) (emphasis added). 
Moreover, the Court found that such a relationship did exist. There, the plaintiffs had “confidence 
in [the defendant]; he was their friend; they all trusted him and believed that he wanted to help 
them live in peace in their home.” Id. at 263, 316 S.E.2d at 275 (emphasis added).

Ms. Griffith found herself in a dire circumstance, and Defendant assumed a position where 
he, as did the defendant in Curl , offered to help Ms. Griffith with her problems and encouraged 
her to trust him and his company—to “turn to us with confidence.” By placing himself in a position 
of trust and confidence with relation to Ms. Griffith —explicitly encouraging her to trust him and 
the company with the process, meeting one -on-one and answering her questions regarding the 
transaction, drafting the deed, organizing the meetings and notary—Defendant not only stepped 
into a fiduciary and confidential relationship with Ms. Griffith, but actively encouraged, cultivated, 
and formulated such a dynamic. See Curl  at 264, 316 S.E.2d at 275. Ms. Griffith had such 
confidence in him that she granted him her Power of Attorney within three (3) days of their first 
phone call. 
Defendants have not provided any evidence that a fiduciary or confidential relationship 
does not exist. Conversely, the overwhelming weight of evidence shows that such a relationship 
exists as both a matter of law and fact.  
C. Because a fiduciary relationship exists between Ms. Griffith and Defendant, the 
burden shifts to the Defendant to provide evidence that the conveyance was not the 
product of a breach of fiduciary relationship, constructive fraud, or undue influence, 
but—rather—the product of an open, fair, and honest transaction.  
Furthermore, once a plaintiff establishes a prima facie case of a fiduciary relationship, “the 
burden shifts to the defendant to prove he acted in an ‘open, fair and honest’ manner, so that no 
breach of fiduciary duty occurred.” Collier  at 432, 719 S.E.2d at 81 (quoting Estate of Smith v. 
Underwood, 127 N.C. App. 1, 9, 487 S.E.2d 807, 812 (1997) (internal quotation omitted). In other 
words, “[w]hen the parties are engaged in a fiduciary relationship, constructive fraud is presumed 
when the ‘superior party obtains a possible benefit.’” Collier  at 432, 719 S.E.2d at 81 (quoting 
===== PDF PAGE 20 =====

20 
 
Watts v. Cumberland County Hosp. System, 317 N.C. 110, 116, 343 S.E.2d 879, 884 (1986) 
(internal citations omitted)). “This presumption arises not so much because [the fiduciary] has 
committed fraud, but [because] he may have done so.” Id. (bracketed terms in original) (emphasis 
added). In determining whether the transaction was “open, fair and honest,” the “superior party 
can rebut the presumption by showing that the confidence reposed in him was not abused, but that 
the other party acted o n independent advice.” Collier at 432, 719 S.E.2d at 81 (internal citations 
omitted). Moreover, where a “grantee or other beneficiary of a deed or will is a person who has 
maintained intimate relations with the grantor or testator, or has drafted, or advised the terms of 
the instrument, a presumption of undue influence or of fraud on the part of the beneficiary has 
often been applied.” McNeill v. McNeill, 223 N.C. 178, 181, 25 S.E.2d 615, 617 (1943) (quoting 
Wigmore on Evidence (3
rd Ed.), sec. 2503, and cases cited in notes) (internal quotations omitted). 
In McNeill, the Supreme Court found that the trial court erred when it failed to instruct the jury to 
apply a “presumption of fraud arising out of the relation of the parties.” McNeill at 183, 25 S.E.2d 
at 618. 
In the present case, Defendant works in the business of acquiring real property under 
foreclosure. Ms. Griffith is an elderly woman with diminished capacity. Defendant openly 
encouraged and courted a fiduciary and confidential relationship with Ms. Griffit h—and even 
became the Power of Attorney over her affairs surrounding the real property. Further, Defendant 
drafted the documents that Ms. Griffith signed, answered questions about the sale and foreclosure 
process, and advised Ms. Griffith about the instruments she was signing. Assuming that such a 
relationship existed, the burden must shift, as a matter of law, to Defendant to show that the 
transaction itself was not due to a breach of fiduciary duty, constructive fraud, or undue influence.

Defendants argue that it is material to the present case that Ms. Griffith signed such 
documents herself rather than Defendant signing such documents on Ms. Griffith’s behalf as her 
agent. However, this argument misses the point. The clear authority of North Carolina law provides 
that the mere existence of a confidential relationship—that a plaintiff only establish a prima facie 
case that such a relationship exists—causes the burden to shift, not because there necessarily was 
a breach, but that there may have been a breach. See Collier at 432, 719 S.E.2d at 81. Because the 
burden has shifted to Defendants, it is therefore Defendants’ obligation to provide sufficient 
evidence at summary judgment to show that the conveyance in question was the product of an 
“open, fair, and honest” transaction. 
D. Because Defendants have not provided any evidence that the conveyance in question 
was the product of an “open, fair, and honest” transaction, Ms. Griffith is entitled to 
Summary Judgment.  
Once the burden shifts to a defendant in a breach of fiduciary duty, constructive fraud, or 
undue influence case, the defendant must show that the conveyance in question was the product of 
an “open, fair, and honest” transaction.  
The inherent unfairness of the transaction is facially obvious from the exchange. Defendant 
paid off the outstanding debt to which the property was subject to of $22,157.08, a negligible 
$100.00 in earnest money , and granted Ms. Griffith a life estate valued at $117,134.21 In total, 
Ms. Griffith received $139,391.29 of consideration as a result of the exchange. Defendants on the 
other hand received a property valued by Defendant himself in excess of $300,000.00. And, while 
Defendants have not yet sold the property, BYH immediately accessed the equity of the home, 
using it as collateral for a $75,000.00 loan. See EXHIBIT K. The burden is on the Defendants to 
===== PDF PAGE 22 =====

show that the transaction was “fair” when Defendant received over $300,000.00 worth of 
consideration in comparison with Ms. Griffith receiving only $139,391.29. 
For such reasons, Ms. Griffith is entitled to summary judgment as a matter of law on the 
issues of breach of fiduciary duty, constructive fraud, and undue influence. 
CONCLUSION 
No material fact exists as to the creation of a fiduciary relationship between Defendant and 
Ms. Griffith, and because this creates a presumption that the transaction was fraudulent, Ms. 
Griffith is entitled to summary judgment on its claims of breach of fiduciary duty, constructive 
fraud, and undue influence because Defendant’s have failed to rebut the presumption by providing 
sufficient evidence that the conveyance was the product of a “open, fair, and honest” transaction.  
Defendants’ own admissions provi de sufficient, irrebuttable evidence that they engaged in an 
Unfair and Deceptive Trade Practice by f acilitating a n illegal Home Foreclosure Rescue 
Transaction by failing to provide the necessary protections to Ms. Griffith.  
For these reasons, Ms. Griffith asks  this Court to grant Summary Judgment in her favor 
and deny Defendants’ Cross Motion for Summary Judgment.
