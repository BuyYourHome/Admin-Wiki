# Brynda Suit Mediation Evidence And Remedies Analysis

Prepared: 2026-07-27
Purpose: Attorney review and mediation preparation
Case: *Griffith v. Browning*, Wake County Superior Court, 24CV037077-910

## Executive Assessment

The strongest defensible mediation points are not that Natalie Doherty committed perjury or knowingly filed a false lawsuit. The record reviewed does not establish either proposition. The strongest points are narrower:

1. Natalie verified and helped initiate a complaint containing material characterizations for which she later admitted she had no firsthand knowledge or could not identify independent factual support.
2. Before suit, Natalie did not read important lease terms, investigate the alleged loan terms, ask Wes to reconsider the transaction, or give him an opportunity to address the dispute.
3. Natalie had little contact with Brynda from 2019 until summer 2024, despite Kevin Huston's recollection that he contacted Natalie in September 2023 and came away believing she was willing to help Brynda financially.
4. The original guardian-ad-litem procedure was set aside after defendants challenged the lack of notice, hearing, and incompetency finding. That is a procedural concern, not proof that Natalie or counsel acted with an improper purpose.
5. The proposed summary-judgment order would dispose of every claim except undue influence. That materially narrows the case, but the surviving claim and Kevin Huston's diminished-capacity testimony prevent a responsible assertion that the entire action lacked any factual basis.

The lawful mediation framing is therefore: defendants have preserved potential statutory fee and sanction requests; defendants have documented defense costs and other losses; and defendants will ask counsel to pursue any remedy supported by the final record. Do not frame this as a personal threat against Natalie.

## Strongest Discrepancies And Unsupported Positions

### 1. "Harsh" solicitation language versus Brynda's description

**Complaint position:** Complaint paragraph 13 characterized Wes's letters as using "harsh language" about the default.
**Competing evidence:** Brynda described Wes's letter as "very pleasant" and said it was the one that attracted her attention. Natalie was asked for the factual basis for "harsh language" and answered, "I don't know."
**Sources:** `25-06-10 DOHERTY, NATALIE - deposition.pdf`, 103:10-19, 104:3-23; `25-03-28 Brynda Griffith 032825_rough draft_cond_N.pdf`, 42:13-25.
**Why it matters:** This is a clean mismatch between a pleaded characterization and the plaintiff's own description of the letter. It supports an argument that the original pleading used advocacy language that Natalie could not substantiate.
**Confidence / limit:** High as to the inconsistency. It does not prove counsel lacked another basis for the wording.

### 2. "Coerced" versus Natalie's admitted understanding of the word

**Complaint position:** Complaint paragraphs 15 and 17 alleged Brynda was coerced into signing transaction documents.
**Natalie's testimony:** Natalie said "coerced" meant "misled" in her mind. After seeing a definition involving force, threat, restraint, or domination, she acknowledged it "seem[ed] different." She did not know the time frame of the alleged yelling or domination and was not present for the transaction. When asked for supporting facts, she answered that she had none other than what Brynda told her and her interpretation of Brynda's deposition.
**Brynda's testimony:** Brynda said she did not recall whether Wes had ever forced her to sign a document and did not understand that her lawsuit alleged coercion.
**Sources:** Natalie deposition 105:16-25, 106:1-24, 107:1-17, 109:18-25, 110:5-24, 133:4-18; Brynda deposition 76:21-25, 77:1-25, 78:15-23, 81:7-20.
**Why it matters:** The verified pleading used a legally and factually stronger word than Natalie's stated understanding and firsthand knowledge supported.
**Confidence / limit:** High that Natalie's basis was limited and partly semantic. Brynda's inability to recall is not affirmative proof that coercion did not occur, and Natalie's report of what Brynda told her remains potential evidence subject to admissibility and credibility issues.

### 3. Intentional "guise" or trick versus no identified contemporaneous representation

**Complaint position:** The complaint alleged defendants acted under a guise or pretense and that Brynda believed the transaction was a loan.
**Natalie's testimony:** Natalie had seen nothing in writing indicating a loan; agreed the signed documents were inconsistent with a mere loan; did not recall Brynda using the exact alleged-loan words; said "we assumed" that was what Brynda meant; did not ask about a payment, interest rate, security, or note; and did not sit down with Brynda to review the documents during the six or seven weeks before suit. She ultimately acknowledged Brynda was wrong when Brynda said no sale occurred.
**Huston's later clarification:** In the 2024 call that prompted his deed search, Huston's takeaway was that Brynda believed she was selling or had sold the property and that the buyer was supposed to pay the mortgage, although she could not explain the terms clearly.
**Sources:** Natalie deposition 116:1-24, 117:1-24, 118:1-24, 119:1-24, 120:1-24, 127:1-24, 128:1-24, 129:1-24, 130:1-24, 131:1-25, 132:1-17; Huston continued deposition, `26-02-10 - Huston Depo Transcript COND_VOL. II.pdf`, 147:11-25, 151:16-25, 152:1-24, 159:9-17.
**Why it matters:** This is the strongest challenge to an intentional-deception theory. It separates Brynda's later confusion from proof of Wes's intent at signing.
**Confidence / limit:** High that the investigation was incomplete and that Huston's testimony undercuts a categorical "loan only" account. It does not resolve whether Brynda understood the nature and effect of the specific Wes transaction in October 2023.

### 4. Natalie's claim of a factual basis despite no firsthand transaction knowledge

**Natalie's testimony:** She did not know what happened between Wes's letters and execution, was not part of those events, and had no firsthand information about Wes's discussions with Brynda. Her opinion that Wes knew Brynda was mentally incapable was not based on being present, and she did not know when Wes learned of Brynda's reported phone-tapping concern.
**Sources:** Natalie deposition 104:24-25, 105:1-15, 133:4-18.
**Why it matters:** It limits Natalie's ability to establish Wes's state of mind or signing conduct from personal knowledge.
**Confidence / limit:** High. Other witnesses, the signing video, documents, or circumstantial evidence may supply facts Natalie lacked.

### 5. "Disinterested" guardian-ad-litem verification versus family benefit

**Verified position:** Natalie's GAL petition stated under oath that she was a disinterested person fit to represent Brynda.
**Competing evidence:** Natalie testified that the requested return of the property would benefit her two daughters under Brynda's will. She remained comfortable calling herself disinterested and had not considered an unrelated GAL.
**Sources:** Natalie deposition 98:8-20, 99:11-24, 100:11-25, 101:1-18.
**Why it matters:** This is a facial conflict worth attorney review concerning what "disinterested" meant in the petition and whether the familial testamentary benefit required disclosure or a different representative.
**Confidence / limit:** Medium. "Disinterested" is a legal term; indirect family benefit does not automatically establish legal disqualification or dishonesty.

### 6. Brynda allegedly made the litigation decision versus her limited understanding

**Natalie's position:** Natalie testified that Brynda made the decision to file the lawsuit.
**Brynda's testimony:** Brynda did not remember reviewing the complaint, did not know why she sued Wes, did not understand the suit exactly, did not know that Buy Your Home was a defendant, and did not understand the coercion allegation. She said Natalie did "everything" for her in the lawsuit and that she stayed out of Natalie's way.
**Sources:** Natalie deposition 101:21-25, 102:1-25, 103:1-4; Brynda deposition 54:7-24, 55:1-18, 58:1-14, 59:1-13, 81:7-20, 82:5-18.
**Why it matters:** It supports careful examination of who authorized particular allegations and the continued prosecution of the case.
**Confidence / limit:** High that the testimony is in tension. Brynda's deposition occurred later and shows substantial memory difficulty, so it does not prove she lacked understanding or authorization when the case was filed.

### 7. Fear narrative versus contemporaneous cordial and assistance-related communications

**Natalie's testimony:** Natalie said Brynda was scared of Wes and did not want to be around him. Natalie admitted she personally observed nothing in Wes's communications or demeanor indicating that concern was legitimate.
**Competing evidence:** In August and early September 2024, Brynda told Natalie that Wes was coming to paint, that Wes would take her to a medical appointment, and sent a cheerful "Good morning Wes and Jenny" message. Brynda also testified that Wes was her emergency contact and "just about the only person" she saw, ordered groceries, took her to the bank and doctors, and responded to a late-night plumbing issue.
**Sources:** Natalie deposition 80:7-21; `Exhibit 21 - Nikki-Brynda Text thread - Aug 11 - Sept 26, 2024.pdf`, PDF pp. 3-4, 15 (Aug. 15, Sept. 4-5 messages); Brynda deposition 59:14-25, 60:1-23, 61:3-18, 62:21-25, 63:1-12.
**Why it matters:** It supplies contemporaneous evidence inconsistent with a simple, continuous fear-and-control narrative.
**Confidence / limit:** Medium. A person can accept help while also feeling afraid, and the texts include later complaints that Wes had not checked on Brynda and got an appointment time wrong. This point should be used as credibility context, not as proof that fear was impossible.

## Potentially Improper Filing Or Pursuit: Attorney-Review Issues

### A. Reasonable inquiry into pleaded facts

The record supports asking counsel whether Rule 11 relief is viable for specific papers or allegations:

- Natalie verified a complaint-related GAL petition while lacking firsthand knowledge of the transaction.
- She could not identify a basis for "harsh language."
- She used "coerced" to mean "misled" and did not know the time frame of the alleged conduct.
- She had no written loan representation, did not investigate ordinary loan terms, and did not review key lease terms.
- She acknowledged the documents contradicted the "no sale" statement.

The counterweight is substantial: Brynda displayed serious memory and comprehension difficulty; Huston opined she had diminished capacity in 2023; Huston described confusion over Duane's offer and difficulty with documents; and one undue-influence count survived the proposed summary-judgment ruling. Those facts make a broad claim that the whole suit was baseless difficult to sustain.

### B. GAL appointment and representative status

The defense motion argued the appointment was void because it occurred without notice, hearing, or a judicial finding of incompetency. The defendants' summary-judgment brief says plaintiff consented to vacatur and the order was set aside. The reviewed record supports describing this as a corrected procedural defect. It does not, by itself, prove bad faith by Natalie or counsel.

### C. Continued pursuit after adverse evidence developed

Attorney review should focus on particular claims and dates:

- When did counsel receive Brynda's deposition testimony that she did not understand the suit or coercion allegation?
- When did counsel receive Natalie's admissions concerning the letters, coercion terminology, loan investigation, lease rider, and lack of firsthand knowledge?
- When did Huston clarify that Brynda appeared to understand foreclosure and sale as a solution, while also testifying to diminished capacity?
- Which claims were voluntarily withdrawn, dismissed, or pursued through summary judgment after those dates?

The proposed order's dismissal of seven counts is relevant evidence, but North Carolina law expressly says summary judgment alone is not sufficient for a fee award under G.S. 6-21.5.

### D. Agency complaints

The North Carolina Real Estate Commission closed its file after finding Wes's response satisfactory and taking no further action.
**Source:** `24-11-14 NC RE Commission Complaint File Closed.pdf`, letter dated 2024-11-14.
This supports a mediation statement that the regulatory complaint produced no discipline. It does not prove that filing the complaint was improper.

## Current North Carolina Remedy Paths

This section is for Keith Johnson's review, not a legal conclusion.

### 1. Rule 11 sanctions

North Carolina Rule of Civil Procedure 11 certifies that a signed paper is well grounded in fact after reasonable inquiry, warranted by law or a good-faith extension argument, and not interposed for an improper purpose. A violation requires an appropriate sanction and may include reasonable expenses and attorney fees caused by the filing. See [N.C. Gen. Stat. Â§ 1A-1, Rule 11](https://ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_1A/GS_1A-1%2C_Rule_11.html).

**Attorney questions:** Which signed papers remain sanction candidates? Who signed or verified each? What fees were caused by each challenged paper? Are there procedural timing or safe-harbor concerns under controlling North Carolina case law?

### 2. Attorney fees for a complete absence of a justiciable issue

G.S. 6-21.5 permits a prevailing party to seek fees when a pleading raised a complete absence of a justiciable issue of law or fact. Summary judgment may be evidence but is not independently sufficient, and the court must make findings. See [N.C. Gen. Stat. Â§ 6-21.5](https://library.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_6/GS_6-21.5.html).

**Assessment:** Potentially stronger claim-by-claim than case-wide. The surviving undue-influence issue and Huston's capacity testimony are major barriers to asserting that the entire case lacked any justiciable issue.

### 3. Fees tied to the dismissed unfair-trade-practice claim

For a suit alleging a violation of G.S. 75-1.1, G.S. 75-16.1 permits a prevailing defendant to seek fees if the plaintiff knew or should have known the action was frivolous and malicious. See [N.C. Gen. Stat. Â§ 75-16.1](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_75/GS_75-16.1.html).

**Attorney questions:** Did the proposed summary-judgment ruling make defendants the prevailing party on the Chapter 75 claim for fee purposes before final judgment? What evidence meets both "frivolous" and "malicious," and what fees are attributable to that claim?

### 4. Fees tied to the punitive-damages claim

G.S. 1D-45 requires reasonable fees resulting from defense of a punitive-damages claim that the claimant knew or should have known was frivolous or malicious. See [N.C. Gen. Stat. Â§ 1D-45](https://www.ncleg.gov/enactedlegislation/statutes/html/bysection/chapter_1d/gs_1d-45.html).

**Attorney questions:** Was punitive relief independently pleaded and disposed of? What work can be allocated specifically to defending that claim? Does the evidence satisfy the statutory knowledge standard?

### 5. Malicious prosecution after favorable termination

North Carolina requires initiation, malice, lack of probable cause, and favorable termination. For a prior civil proceeding, a claimant must also plead and prove special damages. *Fuhs v. Fuhs* explains that ordinary reputation injury, embarrassment, lost work or leisure time, and defense expenses are not special damages; examples that may qualify involve substantial interference with person or property, such as attachment, injunction, receivership, or wrongful commitment. See [*Fuhs v. Fuhs*, 245 N.C. App. 367 (2016)](https://www.nccourts.gov/assets/documents/advanced-sheets/245-3.pdf).

**Assessment:** This theory appears weak on the current record. It cannot mature before favorable termination, and the presently identified monetary defense costs, reputational harm, and time/energy are specifically the kinds of harm *Fuhs* treats as insufficient special damages in a civil malicious-prosecution claim.

### 6. Abuse of process

Abuse of process requires an ulterior purpose and an improper act in the use of process after issuance; filing a case with an allegedly bad motive is not enough. See [*Beroth Oil Co. v. Whiteheart*, 173 N.C. App. 89, 99 (2005)](https://www.nccourts.gov/assets/documents/pdf-volumes/nccoa173.pdf).

**Assessment:** No specific post-issuance misuse of process was established in the reviewed sources. Seeking rescission, damages, discovery, and trial relief are ordinary litigation uses unless evidence shows process was used to obtain an unauthorized collateral objective.

## Recommended Counsel-Reviewed Mediation Framing

Use:

> Defendants believe the discovery record materially undercuts several allegations that have now been dismissed, and they have preserved all rights to seek recoverable fees, sanctions, costs, and other relief supported by the final record. Defendants have documented their litigation expenses and other losses. A complete, confidential resolution now can avoid additional trial expense and post-judgment proceedings for all sides.

Avoid:

- "We will sue Natalie personally after trial."
- "Natalie committed perjury, fraud, or misconduct."
- "The lawsuit was proven baseless."
- A demand for reputational or personal-time damages as though those categories are automatically recoverable.
- Suggesting a sanctions motion will be filed unless Natalie accepts a settlement.

## Bottom Line

The mediation record supports substantial pressure through precise, counsel-reviewed facts: weak pre-suit investigation of several allegations, Natalie's lack of firsthand knowledge, conflicts between complaint language and deposition testimony, delayed family involvement, and dismissal of most claims. It does not yet support a safe assertion of perjury, fraud, malicious prosecution, abuse of process, or guaranteed fee recovery. The strongest immediate leverage is a documented request for final peace that expressly preserves statutory fee and sanction remedies while avoiding personal accusations or threats.
